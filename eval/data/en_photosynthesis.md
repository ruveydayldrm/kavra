# Biology 101 — Week 6: Photosynthesis

## 1. Overview

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. In plants it takes place in chloroplasts, mainly in the cells of the leaf mesophyll. The overall equation is: six carbon dioxide molecules plus six water molecules, using light energy, produce one glucose molecule and six oxygen molecules.

The oxygen released during photosynthesis comes from the splitting of water, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown with experiments using water labelled with the heavy oxygen isotope O-18.

## 2. Chloroplast structure

A chloroplast is surrounded by a double membrane. Inside it are stacks of flattened sacs called thylakoids; a stack of thylakoids is called a granum. The fluid surrounding the thylakoids is the stroma. Chlorophyll is embedded in the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b absorb mainly blue-violet and red light and reflect green light, which is why leaves look green.

## 3. Light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes. Light energy excites electrons in photosystem II, and water is split to replace those electrons, releasing oxygen as a by-product. The electrons move through an electron transport chain to photosystem I. This flow pumps hydrogen ions into the thylakoid space, and ATP synthase uses the resulting gradient to make ATP. At the end of the chain, electrons reduce NADP+ to NADPH. The products of the light-dependent reactions are therefore ATP, NADPH and oxygen.

## 4. The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma and does not use light directly, although it depends on the ATP and NADPH made in the light-dependent reactions. In the first stage, carbon fixation, the enzyme rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called RuBP. In the second stage, ATP and NADPH are used to reduce the products into G3P, a three-carbon sugar. In the third stage, most of the G3P is used to regenerate RuBP. Three turns of the cycle produce one net G3P molecule, and two G3P molecules can be combined to form glucose.

Rubisco is thought to be the most abundant protein on Earth. It can also bind oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, a wasteful process called photorespiration that is more common in hot, dry conditions.

## 5. Limiting factors

The rate of photosynthesis is limited by light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. Increasing light intensity raises the rate until another factor becomes limiting. Because photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes, the rate falls sharply at very high temperatures as the enzymes denature.

C4 and CAM plants have adaptations that reduce photorespiration. CAM plants, such as cacti, open their stomata at night to take in carbon dioxide and keep them closed during the hot day to save water.
